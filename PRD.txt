# LittleVerse AI
## Product Requirements Document (PRD) & Implementation Specification

**Product:** LittleVerse AI  
**Initial module:** Jungle Counting Adventure  
**Product category:** Voice-first, interactive children's learning platform  
**Primary users:** Children aged 3–10, parents, and teachers  
**Initial learning audience:** Nursery and kindergarten, approximately ages 4–6  
**Development environment:** Claude Code  
**Coding model:** Claude Opus 5.5, if available in the configured environment  
**Frontend:** Next.js App Router, React, TypeScript  
**Motion:** Motion for React  
**Charts:** Recharts  
**Backend target:** Supabase  
**Voice AI target:** Bolna AI  
**Document status:** Implementation-ready specification

---

# 1. Executive Summary

LittleVerse AI is an interactive educational platform that transforms early learning into an animated adventure.

Instead of asking children to navigate conventional educational dashboards or interact with a generic chatbot, LittleVerse presents learning activities as a playful world. Children explore illustrated environments, speak with a friendly learning companion, solve age-appropriate challenges, receive supportive feedback, and develop skills through guided practice.

The platform must combine:

1. Immersive, animated educational environments.
2. Natural voice interaction and spoken instructions.
3. Structured educational activities with validated answers.
4. Adaptive hints and progressive difficulty.
5. Meaningful learning-progress tracking.
6. Parent and teacher visibility.
7. Strong child-safety and privacy boundaries.

The first release must focus on one polished vertical slice: **Jungle Counting Adventure**.

The objective is not to produce a collection of attractive static screens. It is to deliver one complete learning experience with functional interactions, an understandable learning loop, persistent-ready data structures, and a coherent visual identity.

## Product vision

Make learning feel like exploring a magical world with a patient companion.

## Core product promise

Every interaction should help a child discover something, practice a skill, or demonstrate understanding.

## Primary success criterion

A child can complete a short counting adventure, receive appropriate feedback, and produce a structured learning result that a parent can inspect.

---

# 2. Problem Statement

Young children often encounter educational products designed around reading, repetitive quizzes, generic videos, or adult-oriented dashboards.

These approaches may not suit children who are still developing reading fluency, attention control, vocabulary, or confidence in answering questions.

Parents and teachers also need a better way to understand what a child actually practiced, rather than relying solely on screen time, points, or completion badges.

LittleVerse addresses these needs through short, interactive, multimodal learning experiences.

## Problems to solve

- Children should not need strong reading skills to begin learning.
- Incorrect answers should trigger useful guidance instead of discouragement.
- Learning should feel interactive rather than passive.
- Difficulty should adapt to demonstrated understanding.
- Parents should be able to see what the child practiced and where support may help.
- Voice AI must remain bounded, reliable, and appropriate for young users.

## Product principles

1. Learning before entertainment.
2. Interaction before passive consumption.
3. Encouragement before correction.
4. Demonstrated understanding before progression.
5. Privacy before personalization.
6. Reliability before feature quantity.
7. Accessible experiences before visual complexity.

# 3. Target Users and Personas

## Persona A: The young learner

**Age:** 4–6 years  
**Abilities:** Developing language, counting, attention, and early reasoning  
**Needs:** Clear instructions, large controls, engaging visuals, positive reinforcement  
**Limitations:** May struggle with reading, typing, long instructions, or precise speech recognition

The child should be able to complete the core adventure through a combination of spoken answers and visual interactions.

## Persona B: The parent

**Needs:**
- Understand what the child learned.
- See which activities were completed.
- Identify skills that may benefit from additional practice.
- Control access, session duration, and learning settings.
- Understand what data is stored.

The parent dashboard should prioritize meaningful learning evidence rather than vanity metrics.

## Persona C: The teacher

**Needs:**
- Review skill-level progress.
- Identify activities that children find difficult.
- Recommend additional practice.
- Inspect activity outcomes without needing access to unrestricted conversations.

Teacher features are part of the product direction, but full classroom management is outside the initial MVP.

The application should have two distinct experiences: a child-facing adventure and an adult-facing dashboard.

## Child-facing routes

- `/` — Explorer home.
- `/adventure/jungle-counting` — Main learning experience.
- `/adventure/jungle-counting/complete` — Activity summary.

## Adult-facing routes

- `/parent` — Parent dashboard.
- `/parent/progress` — Detailed skill progress.
- `/parent/settings` — Learning preferences and privacy settings.

These routes may be implemented in the initial codebase, but the first polished flow should prioritize the explorer home, adventure, and parent dashboard.

## Navigation rules

- Parent routes should be protected in production.
- Use a clearly visible adult-facing exit or parent-access control.
- Do not rely on an easily guessed URL as the sole access control mechanism.
- Keep the child experience visually distinct from the adult dashboard.

# 6. Visual Design System

## Creative direction

Create a premium, whimsical, illustrated storybook experience.

The interface should feel like a carefully art-directed children's adventure game rather than a generic SaaS template.

### Visual characteristics

- Rich illustrated environments.
- Friendly animal characters.
- Layered jungle foliage.
- Soft depth and atmospheric lighting.
- Rounded interactive surfaces.
- Large, clear typography.
- Expressive but restrained animation.
- Strong contrast for important instructions.
- Playful visual feedback tied to learning actions.

### Color palette

Use a consistent token system:

- Forest green: `#205C43`
- Deep jungle: `#123D30`
- Leaf green: `#77B96A`
- Warm sunshine: `#FFD166`
- Sky blue: `#8BD3F7`
- Coral accent: `#FF8A72`
- Cream background: `#FFF8E9`
- Primary text: `#24352B`

These are initial design tokens, not rigid constraints. Validate readability and contrast before finalizing them.

### Typography

Use a friendly, highly legible sans-serif family, such as Nunito or a similar rounded family.

Use:
- Large headings for the child experience.
- Short instructions.
- Generous line spacing.
- High readability at tablet viewing distances.
- A more compact typographic scale for the parent dashboard.

Do not use decorative typefaces for essential instructions.

### Layout

For the child experience:
- Full-screen illustrated environment.
- Central learning activity.
- Large answer controls.
- Prominent voice interaction control.
- Minimal navigation.
- Responsive composition for mobile, tablet, and desktop.

For the parent experience:
- Clear navigation.
- Compact metric cards.
- Progress charts.
- Skill breakdowns.
- Recent activity.
- Helpful next-step recommendations.

### Visual assets

Prefer purpose-created illustrations, lightweight SVG assets, or licensed assets.

Do not depend on random remote image URLs or emoji-only layouts to achieve the finished design.

If custom images are unavailable during the first implementation, build a cohesive placeholder environment using local SVG illustrations, CSS layering, gradients, and simple vector characters.

Do not block the core experience on unavailable external assets.

# 8. Jungle Counting Adventure

This is the core MVP and should receive the most implementation and testing effort.

## Learning objective

The child practices counting objects and associating spoken number words with quantities from 1 to 5.

## Initial session

Target duration: approximately 3–5 minutes.

## Activity sequence

### Challenge 1: Count the butterflies

Display three clearly distinguishable butterflies.

Miko says:

"Look at the butterflies! Can you count them?"

The child can:
- Speak the answer.
- Tap a numbered answer.
- Request a hint.

Expected answer: 3.

On success:
- Miko celebrates briefly.
- The UI confirms the answer.
- The result is recorded.
- The next activity becomes available.

### Challenge 2: Count the bananas

Display five clearly distinguishable bananas.

Miko says:

"How many bananas can you see?"

Expected answer: 5.

If the child struggles, offer a counting hint that highlights objects one at a time.

### Challenge 3: Help the elephants

Display four elephants.

Miko asks:

"Can you help me find four elephants?"

Expected answer: 4.

The child should be allowed to tap each object to count it or select a numbered answer.

### Important implementation requirement

Do not rely on visual counting alone to establish the answer. Each activity must have a structured data definition containing the expected answer and validation rules.

Example conceptual structure:

- Activity ID.
- Skill ID.
- Prompt.
- Visual object type.
- Object count.
- Expected answer.
- Available answer options.
- Hint sequence.
- Completion rule.
- Difficulty level.

## Answer feedback

### Correct answer

- Brief positive reinforcement.
- A restrained celebratory animation.
- A recorded successful attempt.
- Progress to the next challenge.

### Incorrect answer

- Friendly acknowledgement.
- No buzzer, punishment, or shame.
- A progressively clearer hint.
- An opportunity to try again.

Example:

"Good try! Let's count them together."

Then highlight the objects one by one.

Do not tell the child they have mastered a skill simply because they eventually selected the right answer after multiple hints.

## Adaptive hint sequence

1. First incorrect response: encourage another attempt.
2. Second incorrect response: highlight or count the objects together.
3. Continued difficulty: demonstrate the solution and invite the child to repeat the count.
4. Record the result with the appropriate assistance level.

The activity should finish even when the child needs help. Do not trap the child in an endless retry loop.

## Session completion

Display:
- A short congratulatory message.
- Challenges attempted.
- Correct answers.
- Hints used.
- Skills practiced.
- A button to return to the explorer home.
- A parent-facing option to view the learning summary.

Rewards should celebrate effort and learning, not pressure children to continue playing.

# 9. Voice Interaction Requirements

## Product goal

Make voice interaction feel natural while retaining a fully usable visual fallback.

## Initial voice states

Implement an explicit state machine:

- `idle`
- `requesting_permission`
- `listening`
- `processing`
- `speaking`
- `error`
- `unavailable`

The UI must always communicate the current state.

## Initial implementation

First implement the interaction interface and a working typed or tap-based fallback.

The microphone control must not pretend to be connected to an actual voice service.

If voice support is unavailable, show a clear explanation and allow the child to continue using the visual activity.

## Live integration phase

Integrate Bolna only after verifying the appropriate supported interface and API workflow.

Requirements:
- Secure server-side API credentials.
- No exposed provider secrets in client-side code.
- A defined voice-session lifecycle.
- Explicit loading and failure states.
- Appropriate interruption handling.
- Structured activity outcomes.
- Correct mapping between voice answers and the active activity.
- Recovery from timeouts and failed calls.

## Educational control

The LLM may:
- Narrate the story.
- Ask the configured question.
- Provide approved hints.
- Encourage the child.
- Respond naturally to an answer.

The LLM must not:
- Invent activity answers.
- Directly modify learning records without validation.
- Change the curriculum arbitrarily.
- Make unsupported claims about a child's intelligence or development.
- Initiate unrelated conversations outside the configured learning experience.

The learning engine remains responsible for scoring, activity progression, and skill tracking.

# 10. Parent Dashboard

## Objective

Translate activity results into understandable learning evidence.

The dashboard should feel like a polished, professional educational analytics interface.

## Dashboard sections

### 10.1 Overview

Display:
- Activities completed.
- Skills practiced.
- Recent activity.
- Learning time, when reliably measured.
- A suggested next activity.

Do not equate time spent with learning quality.

### 10.2 Learning progress chart

Use Recharts to show progress over multiple sessions.

Requirements:
- Meaningful axes and labels.
- Responsive chart containers.
- Accessible tooltips.
- A clear empty state.
- Consistent chart colors.
- No fabricated historical data in production.

### 10.3 Skill breakdown

Initially support:
- Counting.
- Number recognition.
- Early phonics as a future skill.

Show skill-specific evidence and recent activity outcomes.

Do not present a single percentage as a validated developmental assessment.

### 10.4 Recent activity

For each session, display:
- Date and duration.
- Activity name.
- Challenges attempted.
- Correct responses.
- Hints used.
- Skills practiced.

### 10.5 Recommendations

Generate simple, explainable suggestions from recorded outcomes.

Example:

"Practice counting objects from 1 to 5 with a visual counting activity."

Do not make clinical, psychological, or developmental diagnoses.

## Data integrity

Charts and summaries must derive from the underlying session records.

If data is missing, show an honest empty state rather than invented values.

If demonstration data is used during development, label it clearly as sample data.

# 12. Data Model

The initial local prototype should use typed activity definitions and session state.

The production architecture should support the following entities.

## Learners

- `id`
- `parent_id`
- `display_name` or a pseudonym
- `age_band`
- `preferred_language`
- `created_at`

Collect only the information necessary for the experience.

## Learning activities

- `id`
- `title`
- `skill_id`
- `difficulty`
- `activity_definition`
- `version`

## Learning sessions

- `id`
- `learner_id`
- `activity_id`
- `started_at`
- `completed_at`
- `status`

## Activity attempts

- `id`
- `session_id`
- `challenge_id`
- `answer_type`
- `is_correct`
- `hint_level`
- `attempt_number`
- `created_at`

Avoid storing raw voice recordings by default.

If speech transcription is required, minimize the retained content and apply appropriate retention and access policies.

## Progress aggregation

Calculate progress from validated attempts.

The scoring implementation must distinguish:
- Correct without assistance.
- Correct after a hint.
- Correct after a demonstration.
- Incorrect or incomplete.

Do not label a child as having mastered a skill based on a single successful answer.

# 13. Motion Design Specification

Use Motion for React to create a consistent motion language.

## Character animation

Support:
- Gentle idle movement.
- A listening state.
- A thinking state.
- A speaking state.
- Short success celebrations.
- Supportive reactions after an incorrect answer.

## Interface transitions

Use motion for:
- Entering an adventure.
- Revealing the next challenge.
- Highlighting countable objects.
- Showing hints.
- Updating progress.
- Completing the activity.

## Performance requirements

- Prefer transforms and opacity for transitions.
- Avoid excessive simultaneous animation.
- Avoid unnecessary animation loops.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Do not animate every background object continuously.
- Avoid blocking input while decorative animations run.

## Accessibility

All animations must be supplementary to the learning task.

Every critical interaction must remain understandable when animations are disabled.

# 14. Child Safety, Privacy and Accessibility

Safety is a core product requirement.

## Content safety

- Keep the tutor within the configured learning activity.
- Use predefined educational content and validated answers.
- Redirect unsuitable or unrelated requests appropriately.
- Do not ask children for sensitive personal information.
- Do not encourage secrecy from parents or caregivers.
- Do not simulate emotional dependency.
- Provide a clear route to a trusted adult for concerning disclosures.

## Privacy

- Parent-managed onboarding and consent.
- Minimal learner data collection.
- No unnecessary voice-recording retention.
- Server-side secret management.
- Appropriate database authorization.
- Defined data-retention and deletion procedures.
- No public learner profiles or leaderboards in the MVP.

## Accessibility

- Large touch targets.
- Keyboard support for adult-facing interfaces.
- Clear focus indicators.
- Text alternatives for essential visual information.
- Captions or text equivalents for important spoken instructions.
- High contrast for text and essential controls.
- Reduced-motion support.
- Visual fallback for voice input.

The application must not assume that every child can hear, speak, read, or interact in the same way.

# 15. Testing Strategy

## Functional tests

Verify:
- The adventure starts.
- Every challenge displays the expected objects.
- Correct answers are validated.
- Incorrect answers produce the right hint.
- Hints progress in the correct order.
- Repeated taps cannot accidentally skip activities.
- Completion produces a correct summary.
- The parent dashboard reads the correct session data.

## Accessibility tests

Verify:
- Keyboard navigation.
- Screen-reader labels.
- Focus management.
- Reduced-motion behavior.
- Touch target sizes.
- Text contrast.

## Reliability tests

Verify:
- No microphone permission.
- Voice service unavailable.
- Network interruption.
- Duplicate submission.
- Page refresh during an activity.
- Empty progress data.
- Invalid activity IDs.
- Repeated answer submissions.

## Educational correctness

Every activity should have explicit learning objectives and known expected answers.

Incorrect responses must not be counted as correct simply because the child eventually completes the activity.

## Acceptance tests

The MVP is acceptable when:

1. A user can start and complete the counting adventure.
2. All three challenges work correctly.
3. Visual answer selection works without voice integration.
4. The hint system responds to incorrect answers.
5. The activity produces a structured session summary.
6. The parent dashboard displays real session results or clearly labelled sample data.
7. The graphs respond to their underlying data.
8. The UI works on mobile, tablet, and desktop.
9. No visible control is knowingly left nonfunctional.
10. There are no exposed secrets or misleading voice-connection states.
11. There are no critical console errors.
12. The README documents setup, scripts, environment variables, and limitations.

# 17. Definition of Done

The first release must not be declared complete merely because the home page looks attractive.

The release is complete only when:

- The child can begin a learning adventure.
- The activity accepts and validates answers.
- The child receives helpful feedback.
- The system records the outcome.
- The completion screen reflects the session.
- The parent dashboard presents the same session's data.
- The application remains usable when voice is unavailable.
- The product respects the specified safety and accessibility requirements.

Any unimplemented production integration must be explicitly documented.

Never claim a live integration, database persistence, or voice interaction works unless it has actually been implemented and tested.
